# Case study: Helping product teams become confident documentation contributors

Documentation self-service takes more than giving someone repository access and sending them instructions.

Product managers know their features, but that doesn't mean they know how to navigate a documentation repo, work through a pull request, interpret a failed build, or get a change published. Those unfamiliar steps can turn a small update into something they put off or hand back to a writer.

Across Partner Center, Marketplace, Microsoft Sales Experience, Power BI, and Fabric, I helped contributors get comfortable with that process. I combined written guidance, walkthroughs, training sessions, office hours, and hands-on support, then applied those practices when teams moved to owning their documentation more independently.

## Starting with the friction

Willingness to contribute wasn't always the problem. People could get stuck between knowing what needed changing and knowing how to make the change.

I encouraged product managers to use our internal web-based WYSIWYG tools and GitHub directly, helped them through unfamiliar steps, and answered questions as they worked on real updates.

I also offered help before someone asked. Monitoring open pull requests let me spot contributors who appeared stuck and help them move forward before their changes became harder to merge.

The goal was to make contributing manageable, especially for people who only needed to do it occasionally.

## Making guidance useful

I created and expanded contributor guidance for the teams I supported. For Partner Center, that included deeper walkthroughs explaining how to contribute without relying on a dedicated content developer.

Written guidance gave people something to return to after a conversation or training session. Live walkthroughs let me fill in the gaps that instructions alone hadn't resolved.

Some contributors needed a quick answer so they could finish a PR. Others needed someone to walk through the workflow and explain how the pieces fit together.

I reinforced the practices through small reminders, examples, and repeated help. Confidence grew through completing actual work.

## Moving toward self-service

The shift was already happening before Partner Center and Marketplace formally moved to product-group ownership.

By spring 2025, **63 product managers had independently managed 854 of 1,332 pull requests—64% of the total**. The teams had practical experience with self-service before it became their primary operating model.

When documentation support transitioned to the product group, I expanded the contributor guidance, explained responsibilities and support paths, and helped prepare vendor writers for their additional work. The existing intake process stayed in place, giving stakeholders continuity while ownership changed.

Microsoft Sales Experience involved a similar need to distribute responsibility. When vendor support ended earlier in that portfolio, I helped transition work to product managers through training and oversight. Later, when the portfolio moved to another content team, I provided training and continued answering questions during the handoff.

These were different transitions, but the same principle applied: people needed to understand both how to make a change and what they were now responsible for maintaining.

## Scaling support through lunch-and-learn sessions

In Power BI and Fabric, I contributed to the v-team's contributor lunch-and-learn sessions. Live sessions complemented the written guidance and day-to-day support. A presentation introduced the process; walkthroughs and follow-up help supported contributors as they applied it to their own changes.

## Results

The clearest result was that more stakeholders were doing the work themselves.

In Partner Center, product managers independently managed nearly two-thirds of pull requests before the formal handoff. The transition built on contribution habits already in place.

That approach continued in Power BI and Fabric. One product partner progressed from a novice contributor to publishing **seven complex capacity-guidance articles within three months**, crediting my guidance with helping him navigate the process.

Those articles were his work. My contribution was helping him become capable of publishing them.

## What I learned

The transitions also showed me where my approach needed to improve.

I had handled some recurring tasks myself because it was quicker and familiar. During the Partner Center handoff, that left gaps in what the receiving team and vendors knew about the full role.

My initial transition plan covered the essentials, but it didn't capture every ongoing responsibility. Some questions only surfaced after I had moved to another portfolio.

The lesson was to make the less-visible work explicit: monitoring stalled PRs, checking build issues, maintaining content, and knowing when to ask for help. A successful handoff needs that operational detail alongside the contribution instructions.

## Why it matters

Contributor enablement helps subject-matter experts turn their knowledge into maintained customer guidance.

I helped people complete their immediate changes while building the confidence and understanding to handle the next ones more independently. That distributed documentation ownership and helped teams continue working as staffing, responsibilities, and organizational structures changed.
